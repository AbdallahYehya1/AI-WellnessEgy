Healthy communication (CDC). Healthy communication is a balancing act in any relationship. Healthy communication means feeling heard, understood, and respected while also being able to listen to, understand, and respect what the other person is saying. How well we communicate has a major impact on our interactions with others. Communication skills affect our ability to prevent or resolve conflict, connect, and build or break trust in a relationship.

Stay in control of your feelings (CDC). When we are feeling strong emotions, such as anger, it is often hard to express ourselves in a way that will not be misunderstood or ignored by the other person. It is important to first calm down.

Eye contact and body language (CDC). One of the best ways to show you are focused on the conversation is to really focus on the person who is speaking. It's not a staring contest but a way to let the speaker know that you value what he or she is saying. Facial expressions and body language send as much of a message as the words that you say. Keep your body relaxed, open, and turned toward the speaker. Make the other person feel welcome and safe through encouraging gestures like smiling, nodding your head, and leaning forward.

Stay calm; think about how you react (CDC). Speak in a respectful, calm, and positive manner that encourages further conversation. If you raise your voice or sound sarcastic, the other person may be less likely to listen to you. Try to avoid criticizing or dismissing the other person's viewpoint. Instead, be constructive.

Listen actively (CDC). Try to recap what the other person has just said. Use statements such as "It sounds like you are saying..." or "I think what you are saying is..." to show that you have been listening and want an accurate understanding of the conversation. Take turns talking. Don't let one person dominate the conversation. Make sure that both people have a chance to express their views and are able to do so without being interrupted.

Ask questions and don't jump to conclusions (CDC). To avoid receiving one-word answers, use open-ended questions like "How do you feel about...?" and "What do you think about...?" When it's appropriate and you need something clarified, ask for further explanation. Try not to assume that you know what the other person needs or how he/she feels before that person has the opportunity to express it. By taking time to hear the other person's perspective, you might learn that the disagreement (if there is one) was all a misunderstanding.

Express your feelings (CDC). Use "I" statements to avoid blaming the other person or making him/her feel defensive. Be specific. Examples of what you could say include: "I feel _____ when you _____ because _____." and "I am _____ and need _____ to ______."
